What you will practise

Technique first, then kata, then years of it.

You'll spend a lot of time on a small number of things until your body starts doing them properly without being told. There's real fighting content in this system. You won't be doing any of it in your first month, and that's on purpose.

01

Learn how to move

We start with how you stand, how your weight shifts, how you step, how you hit. Honestly, most of the first few lessons are spent on things that don't look like karate yet.

02

Learn the forms

Once you can move, we start on kata. These are the forms that hold the system together, and they give you something real to work on at home between lessons.

03

Keep going

Partner work, weapons and sparring come later, once you've got the control for them. There's a ten-kyu and five-dan curriculum behind this. You won't run out of material.

Training safely

Control comes before contact.

You'll work on your own movement, technique and kata first. Partner work, sparring and weapons come later, when you're ready for them rather than after some fixed number of weeks.

Tell me when something hurts and we stop. That's the only rule I'm strict about.

Common questions

What new students usually ask.

Am I too old for this?

No. I've taught people in their nineties. We work from what your body can do now, not from what a twenty-year-old's can. If you've got a health condition, have a word with your doctor first.

Will I get hurt?

Any physical training carries some risk. But you won't be hitting anyone or getting hit, especially early on, and most of what you'll do is work on your own movement. Tell me when something hurts and we stop.

Will it work?

There's real fighting material in this system. I'm not going to promise you'll be able to defend yourself in six weeks, because that isn't how it works. Skill comes from good mechanics, a lot of repetition, and eventually testing things with a partner who knows what they're doing.

What happens in the first lesson?

We'll talk about what you want and anything I should know about your body. Then I'll watch how you stand and move, we'll work through a few fundamentals, and you'll leave with something to practise. Wear whatever you'd wear to the gym and bring water.

What does it cost?

$140 for a ninety-minute lesson, or $500 for four. The first conversation is free. No contract, no registration fee, nothing to buy.

How often should I train?

Every week or two works well. Kata rewards practice on your own, so the lesson is for correction and new material rather than supervised drilling.

Where do lessons happen?

Anywhere within about half an hour of Vaughan City Hall, which covers Maple, Woodbridge, Concord, Thornhill and Richmond Hill. Your place, outdoors when the weather's good, or somewhere booked.

Do I need a gi?

No. My teacher didn't use one and neither do I. Wear comfortable athletic clothes.

Why don't beginners spar?

Because contact needs control, and control takes a while. You'll get there when you're ready for it.

Where does the system come from?

My teacher learned it in Okinawa in the 1940s and passed it on to me. Ryujin-te is the name I gave it. There's a full account on the research page, including the parts I can't prove.
